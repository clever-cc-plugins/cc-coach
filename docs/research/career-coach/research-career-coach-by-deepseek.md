# Evidence Base for One-on-One Career Coaching Conversations

## 1. The GROW Model: Origin, Popularity, and Evidence

### Origin

The GROW model (Goal, Reality, Options, Will) was developed independently in the 1980s by three figures: Graham Alexander, Alan Fine, and Sir John Whitmore. Whitmore popularised it in his 1992 book _Coaching for Performance_, and the model became the dominant framework in corporate coaching, particularly for novice coaches. The model structures coaching conversations as a linear sequence: setting a Goal, examining the current Reality, generating Options, and establishing the Will to act.

### What the Research Shows

**Evidence base is thinner than popularity suggests.** Despite being "probably the best known and most used coaching model", the formal evidence base for GROW is modest relative to its widespread adoption. A meta-analysis of GROW-based interventions reported a pooled effect size of 0.43 (moderate effectiveness), but noted that **effectiveness remains "shaped by contextual factors"**. This heterogeneity suggests that GROW's effectiveness depends heavily on how and in what context it is applied.

**Controlled studies exist but are limited.** Several randomized controlled trials have investigated GROW-based interventions:

- A 2023 RCT (N=77 school administrators) found that a GROW-model cognitive behavioural coaching intervention significantly decreased burnout symptoms—including emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and reduced professional efficacy—and significantly improved leadership self-efficacy, with effects sustained at three-month follow-up. Limitations: small sample size and reliance on self-report measures.
- A Zoom-based GROW intervention RCT with 109 school administrators found positive effects on subjective well-being.
- A primary care RCT compared face-to-face and telephone GROW-based coaching for anxiety and depression.

**However, the comparative evidence is concerning.** A 2026 experimental study (N=592) comparing GROW, Solution-Focused, and CBT coaching chatbots found that the CBT-based chatbot **outperformed both GROW and Solution-Focused chatbots** across most measures of goal attainment, working alliance, and client adoption. The authors note that "there appears to be **no difference in human coaching outcomes based on different approaches**", suggesting that GROW's popularity may reflect marketing and tradition rather than demonstrated superiority.

**Criticisms of GROW are substantive:**

- **Over-simplicity:** The model assumes clients can move through the four stages in a "fairly linear way," which does not match how many clients' cognitive processes actually work.
- **Surface-level exploration:** It focuses on goals but "neglects deeper issues like limiting beliefs" and "misses the broader context of change".
- **Goal-driven bias:** The model "wasn't built for depth, complexity or identity-level work".
- **Limited systemic perspective:** GROW focuses on individuals while "ignoring larger contexts".

**Bottom line:** GROW has some empirical support, but its evidence base is thinner than its popularity suggests. It appears moderately effective in structured, goal-oriented contexts but may be insufficient for complex career transitions, identity work, or clients with non-linear thinking styles. AI coaching tools should be cautious about over-relying on GROW as the sole conversational architecture.

## 2. Broader Coaching Psychology Research on Effective Coaching Conversations

### The Working Alliance

The single most robust finding in coaching psychology is the importance of the **working alliance**—the collaborative bond between coach and client encompassing agreement on goals, tasks, and the relational bond itself.

A meta-analysis synthesising 27 samples (N=3,563 coaching processes) found a **moderate and consistent overall relationship between working alliance and coaching outcomes (r = .41, 95% CI [.34, .48], p < .001)**. Working alliance was positively related to all desirable coaching outcomes (range r = .32 to .64), with the strongest relationships to affective and cognitive outcomes. Importantly, working alliance was **negatively related to unintended negative effects of coaching (r = –.29)**.

These findings held regardless of client type, coach expertise, number of sessions, or whether the alliance was rated by client or coach. A separate meta-analysis of working alliance in individual career counselling (18 studies, N not specified) found a weighted mean effect size of r = .42, with stronger effects for client-perceived quality of the intervention (r = .62) than for career outcomes (r = .28) or mental health outcomes (r = .18).

**Implications for AI coaching:** The working alliance is typically built through human connection—empathy, shared understanding, and trust. AI coaching tools face a fundamental challenge in replicating this. The 2026 chatbot study found that "AI-mediated coaching may activate different psychological processes than human coaching", raising "critical questions about the unique contribution of human coaches in an era of increasingly capable AI coaching tools".

### Agenda-Setting

Agenda-setting is a central communicative task in coaching and "the first opportunity for client-centeredness, which is a central characteristic and success factor for the working alliance". Research shows that "setting and managing the agenda contributes to (initiating) the working alliance of coach and client, which in turn positively influences the overall success of the encounter". Joint agenda-setting "can be shown to be 'doing' working alliance at the same time".

### Goal-Setting Theory (Locke & Latham) in Live Conversation

Locke and Latham's goal-setting theory emphasises **specific, challenging goals** to enhance motivation and performance. However, in live coaching conversations, the application differs from written planning:

- Goals must emerge collaboratively, not be imposed
- The act of articulating goals aloud—with real-time coach responsiveness—may enhance commitment beyond written goal-setting
- Goal clarity in conversation depends on the coach's questioning skill

The working alliance meta-analysis found that alliance was "positively related to all desirable coaching outcomes" including goal attainment, suggesting that the relational context of goal-setting matters as much as the goals themselves.

### Powerful Questioning

Research on coaching questioning practices is emerging. Analysis of authentic coaching data shows that **83% of coaching interactions involve questioning sequences**, and clients "provide (more than) answers in 73% of cases". Questioning sequences—particularly those that combine formulation and question—can "invite self-reflection processes in the client and elicit change". The mechanism appears to be perspective-shifting: questions that change the client's vantage point on their own situation.

**Implications for AI coaching:** AI tools must be designed to simulate the reflective questioning that human coaches use to build alliance and elicit self-generated insight—not merely to provide answers.

## 3. Evidence-Based Practice for the "Options" Stage

### The Divergence-Convergence Distinction

The Options stage requires **divergent thinking**—generating many possibilities—before **convergent thinking**—evaluating and selecting. The core risk is **premature convergence**: jumping to "the answer" without exploring alternatives. Research from creative problem-solving (Osborn-Parnes CPS model) formalises this as alternating phases of divergent and convergent thinking.

### Avoiding Coach Bias

The greatest threat in the Options stage is the coach's **own suggestions crowding out the client's agency**. As one coaching guide notes, "The Options stage is where coaching visibly separates itself from consultancy, mentoring, and advice-giving. This is the moment where the coach deliberately steps back — far removed from because contribution is lacking, and more attached to because client agency must lead".

Research-based techniques to avoid premature convergence and coach bias include:

- **Brainwriting:** Silent idea generation before discussion—reduces anchoring bias
- **Round-robin forced connections:** Each participant builds on the previous idea in unexpected ways
- **"Crazy 8s":** Generating 8 radically different solutions in rapid succession, designed to break anchoring bias
- **Assumption reversal:** Listing every assumption about the problem and reversing them
- **Domain pivoting:** Every 10 ideas, consciously pivot to an orthogonal domain (e.g., UX → Business → Physics → Social Impact)
- **Quantity-before-quality:** The coach's goal "should not be to find the right answer, but to help your coachee identify as many different ideas and solutions as possible," removing obstacles like preferences, feasibility, or completeness

### What the Research Shows

A 2022 review notes that "many leaders tend to provide their own ideas too early in the problem-solving process. Hearing options from someone else can cause the mind to revert back to convergent thinking". Coaches should therefore:

1. Explicitly separate idea generation from idea evaluation
2. Use structured techniques (brainwriting, round-robin) to reduce social influence
3. Ask generative questions rather than offering suggestions
4. Resist the urge to fill silence with their own ideas

**Implications for AI coaching:** AI tools can enforce divergence-convergence discipline more consistently than human coaches, who may default to advice-giving. The challenge is maintaining client agency while using structured ideation prompts.

## 4. Evidence-Based Practice for the "Will" Stage / Commitment Devices

### The Intention-Behaviour Gap

A foundational finding in motivation science: **goal intentions explain only 20–30% of the variance in actual behaviour**. There is a "substantial gap between even strong intentions and subsequent action". This is precisely what the "Will" stage must address.

### Implementation Intentions (Gollwitzer)

**Implementation intentions**—if-then plans that specify _when, where, and how_ a behaviour will occur—are the most robustly supported commitment device in the psychological literature.

Gollwitzer and Brandstätter demonstrated that forming implementation intentions significantly increases follow-through on intended behaviours. The mechanism is automaticity: the if-then plan creates a mental association between a situational cue and a response, such that the behaviour is triggered automatically when the cue is encountered.

A comprehensive meta-analysis of **642 tests** of implementation intentions "underlines the utility of implementation intentions in helping people regulate their thoughts, feelings, and actions". Implementation intentions "facilitate the goal achievement beyond goal-setting by itself".

### Practical Application in Coaching

Implementation intentions work best when people are **highly motivated** to reach a goal but **can't get started or become overwhelmed** by the scope of the task. Coaches should help clients form if-then plans such as:

- "If it is 9am on Monday, then I will spend 30 minutes on my CV"
- "If I receive a rejection email, then I will schedule one networking coffee chat"
- "If I feel anxious about applying, then I will review my accomplishment list"

The plans should specify both the behaviour and the cue for the behaviour. Forming an implementation intention "could be thought of as making a commitment to oneself to carry out a behaviour".

### Anticipating Obstacles

Gollwitzer's research also shows that when people "look into the future and anticipate challenges to ongoing goal achievement and put in place ahead of time some alternative actions in response to the anticipated 'trigger', they were much more likely to maintain commitment to the goal".

**Implications for AI coaching:** The Will stage is perhaps the most amenable to AI support. Structured if-then plan generation, automated reminders, and obstacle-anticipation prompts can be implemented reliably in an AI tool.

## 5. Career-Coaching-Specific Research

### Meta-Analytic Evidence

Individual career counselling has substantial empirical support. A random-effects meta-analysis of 35 independent samples found a **weighted mean effect size of g = 0.82 for career outcomes and g = 0.68 for mental health outcomes**. For college students specifically, career coaching programmes show an overall effect size of 0.727.

Online career counselling interventions also show large effects: a meta-analysis of 14 studies (N=1,000+) found a **large overall effect (Hedges' g = 1.650)** on career-related psychological outcomes including career adaptability.

### Career Transitions and Identity Work

Career transitions are distinct from general coaching because they involve **identity work**—re-evaluating who one is professionally. Evidence suggests that "specifically addressing identity work in coaching leaders, teams and groups significantly increases the success rate of transitions".

The MAP (Me-As-a-Process) coaching model, developed from research with 53 women in voluntary career transitions, identifies four stages of identity work in career change. This research "supports an identity-based approach to coaching career choices and transitions".

### Job Search Coaching

Evidence on job search coaching is mixed but generally positive:

- A study of long-term unemployed job seekers found that coached participants had **higher reemployment rates and lower levels of self-blame** than a control group.
- However, a large-scale evaluation of a digital job search coach in France found **no impact on employment outcomes** over 18 months, suggesting that digital coaching may need to combine with specific job recommendations to be effective.
- A mentoring intervention for vocational students found that mentored students were **27% less likely to exit the labour force, worked 8% more days, were 15% more likely to be employed in their training sector, and earned 18% more** after one year. The study "highlights the power of combining realistic feedback with motivational support to sustain job search effort".

### Working Alliance in Career Counselling

The first meta-analysis of working alliance in individual career counselling (18 studies) confirmed that **working alliance is associated with career counselling effectiveness** (r = .42). The relationship was strongest for client-perceived quality of the intervention (r = .62). Career counsellors should "emphasize on the working alliance during the career counselling process".

**Implications for AI coaching:** Career coaching in an AI tool should:

- Address identity and meaning, not just tactical job-search steps
- Combine structured guidance with motivational support
- Recognise that career transitions are emotionally and identity-laden, not purely rational

## 6. Networking Research Beyond Practitioner Advice

### Granovetter's "Strength of Weak Ties"

Mark Granovetter's 1973 paper "The Strength of Weak Ties" is among the most cited papers in the social sciences, with over 65,000 citations. The core insight: **weak ties** (acquaintances, not close friends) are more valuable for job mobility because they provide access to novel information that strong ties (who share redundant information) cannot. Granovetter found that only 16.7% of workers learned about jobs through strong ties.

### Recent Experimental Evidence

A landmark 2022 study in _Science_ provided **causal experimental evidence** for the strength of weak ties, using multiple large-scale randomised experiments on LinkedIn's "People You May Know" algorithm, covering over 20 million people over 5 years, during which 2 billion new ties and 600,000 new jobs were created.

Key findings that go beyond the original theory:

1. **Non-linear relationship:** The relationship between tie strength and job transmission is **inverted U-shaped**—weaker ties increase job transmission but only to a point, after which there are diminishing marginal returns.

2. **Measurement matters:** Moderately weak ties (measured by **mutual connections**) and the weakest ties (measured by **interaction intensity**) created the most job mobility. Specifically, people with **only 10 mutual connections** proved much more productive for job hunting than stronger contacts with more than 20 mutual connections.

3. **Industry variation:** Weak ties increased job mobility more in digital industries; strong ties increased job mobility more in less digital industries.

### Beyond Granovetter: Social Capital and Network Structure

Organisational behaviour research has extended these insights:

- **Structural holes:** Individuals who bridge disconnected parts of a network (i.e., connect people who don't know each other) have greater access to novel information and career opportunities.
- **Network diversity:** The advantage of weak ties is that they "span a much more diverse network and act as bridges to different parts of that network".
- **Diminishing returns:** Large-scale LinkedIn experiments found that "weak ties boost job mobility up to a point," after which additional weak ties offer less benefit.

### What This Adds Beyond Practitioner Advice

Practitioner networking advice often emphasises:

- Give-first reciprocity (which has some support but limited rigorous testing)
- Cultivate weak ties (consistent with evidence)
- Find "anchor" connectors (supported by structural holes theory)

**What research adds:**

1. **Precision on tie strength:** Not all weak ties are equal—moderately weak ties (around 10 mutual connections) may be optimal.
2. **Diminishing returns:** More networking is not always better—there is an optimal point.
3. **Industry context matters:** The value of weak vs. strong ties varies by industry.
4. **Causal evidence:** The LinkedIn experiments provide causal (not just correlational) evidence.
5. **Structural positioning:** The value of a contact depends not just on relationship strength but on their position in the broader network.

**Implications for AI coaching:** An AI networking coach should:

- Help clients map their network structure, not just count contacts
- Suggest cultivation of moderately weak ties, not just "more networking"
- Tailor advice to industry context
- Recognise diminishing returns and help clients focus effort efficiently
