# The Evidence Base and Best Practices for One-on-One Career Coaching Conversations

## TL;DR
- Coaching "works" as a general intervention (meta-analytic Hedges' *g* ≈ 0.4–0.66 across outcomes), but the specific structural models and techniques coaches sell — above all the GROW model — have far thinner *direct* evidence than their ubiquity implies; the strongest evidence is for *ingredients* (working alliance, solution-focused questioning, implementation intentions, progress monitoring) rather than *brands*.
- For an AI coaching tool, the highest-confidence, best-evidenced moves are: build a task/goal-focused working alliance; use solution-focused rather than problem-focused questioning; help the client self-generate options while the coach scrupulously avoids anchoring them; and convert intentions into if-then implementation-intention plans with public, recorded progress monitoring.
- Career-specific and networking research adds concrete, well-supported guidance: job-search interventions raise the odds of re-employment ~2.67× when they combine skill-building AND motivation; moderately-weak ties and reactivated dormant ties beat close ties for job leads; and people systematically underestimate (by ~48%) how willing others are to help — a bias an AI coach can directly correct.

## Key Findings
1. **GROW is a useful conversational scaffold with almost no direct controlled evidence.** The one experimental test comparing goal-setting-structured coaching against unstructured coaching (Scoular & Linley, 2006) found *no* significant outcome difference. This is "little research conducted," not "proof it fails."
2. **The coaching relationship (working alliance) is the most consistent outcome predictor**, and within it the *task and goal* components matter more than the emotional *bond* (de Haan et al., 2016).
3. **Solution-focused questions reliably outperform problem-focused questions** on affect, self-efficacy, and goal progress across multiple randomized experiments (Grant, 2012 and replications).
4. **In the Options stage, the coach's own suggestions are a liability**: anchoring and advice-taking research shows a facilitator's ideas contaminate the client's option set even when useless; independent idea generation (nominal/brainwriting style) beats interactive suggestion.
5. **In the Will stage, implementation intentions (if-then plans; *d* ≈ 0.65) and progress monitoring (*d* ≈ 0.40, stronger when public/recorded) are among the best-evidenced behavior-change tools in psychology.**
6. **Career-specific and networking findings are actionable and well-sourced**, with important caveats about where practitioner lore outruns evidence (e.g., the "networking feels dirty" finding failed a preregistered replication).

## Details

### 1. The GROW Model — Origin, Attribution, and What the Evidence Actually Shows

**Origins and attribution.** GROW (Goal, Reality, Options, Will/Way Forward) emerged in the UK in the 1980s among a group of business coaches working with McKinsey, and was popularized by Sir John Whitmore's *Coaching for Performance* (1st ed. 1992), which has sold well over a million copies. The intellectual lineage runs through Timothy Gallwey's *The Inner Game of Tennis* (1974); Whitmore and Graham Alexander brought Gallwey's "Inner Game" method to Europe. Attribution is genuinely contested. Whitmore repeatedly stated he was "just the first person to publish it" and that it "originally emerged in a discussion between several coaches with whom I was working at the time, including Graham Alexander, in the McKinsey office in London" (foreword to Wilson, 2007). Alexander has claimed to be the originator ("I am the originator of GROW... in spite of other claims"); Whitmore reportedly conceded "Graham's claim to have invented GROW is technically true." Alan Fine (founder of InsideOut Development) says he co-developed it, distinguishing the four-step *model* from the *GROW labels*, which he attributes to a McKinsey communications specialist. The acronym itself is often credited to Max Landsberg (then at McKinsey) in conversation with Alexander. The three co-developers even diverged on the "W": Whitmore = "Will" (Whitmore, 2009); Alexander = "Wrap-up" (Alexander & Renshaw, 2005); Fine = "Way Forward" (Fine, 2010). Bottom line: GROW is best described as co-created in the mid-to-late 1980s by Whitmore, Alexander, and Fine, inspired by Gallwey, with the acronym coined at McKinsey.

**Effectiveness evidence — thin relative to ubiquity.** This is the crucial honest point. There is a near-total absence of controlled trials testing GROW *specifically* (as opposed to coaching-in-general studies that happened to use GROW as a protocol). The *SAGE Handbook of Coaching* notes little specific research has been conducted on the GROW model. One practitioner-scholar summary states plainly: "There has been little to no empirical research conducted on the use of coaching models and the effectiveness of session structure... The large number of anecdotal reports... does not serve to support its effectiveness from an evidence-based, scientific standpoint."

The single most-cited controlled test is **Scoular and Linley (2006)**, "Coaching, goal-setting and personality type: What matters?" (*The Coaching Psychologist*, 2(1), 9–11). In an experimental between-subjects design (N = 117 coachees, ~14 coaches) with two conditions — goal-setting vs. non-goal-setting — under standardized procedures, with outcomes retested at two and eight weeks, they found (in their words) that "surprisingly, given the extensive research basis for goal-setting, multiple regression analyses showed no significant difference between goal-setting and non-goal-setting conditions. Personality difference was, however, found to be significant" — coaching was *more* effective when coach and coachee had *different* MBTI preferences. Note this is a brief three-page report, not a full-length article.

A handful of recent studies use GROW as the *intervention brand* but do not isolate GROW's structure as the causal ingredient: a 2022 randomized controlled trial of Zoom-delivered "Z-GROW" coaching for 109 Nigerian school administrators found significantly improved subjective well-being sustained at three-month follow-up; a small (N = 14) nursing-student peer-tutoring study reported benefits that may be attributable to peer tutoring rather than GROW itself; and a claimed GROW meta-analysis in education (2026, effect ≈ 0.43) exists but is in low-visibility outlets and does not separate GROW from general coaching. There is also a "GROWS" extension adding behavioral-change elements (Panchal & Riddell, 2020).

**Verdict:** GROW's evidentiary status is "little research conducted," NOT "evidence of no effect." It is a defensible, memorable *scaffold* for structuring a conversation, and general coaching (which usually embeds GROW-like structure) is effective. But claims that GROW *itself* drives outcomes are unsupported; the one direct test found its central mechanism (goal-setting structure) made no measurable difference. An AI tool should treat GROW as a flexible container and load it with the better-evidenced ingredients described below.

### 2. What Makes a Coaching Conversation Effective (Broader Coaching-Psychology Research)

**Overall effectiveness.** Multiple meta-analyses converge that coaching produces positive individual-level effects. Theeboom, Beersma & van Vianen (2014, *Journal of Positive Psychology*, k = 18) reported an overall Hedges' *g* = 0.66, with performance/skills *g* = 0.60, well-being *g* = 0.46, coping *g* = 0.43, work attitudes *g* = 0.54, and goal-directed self-regulation *g* = 0.74; within-subject designs inflated effects (*g* = 1.15) relative to controlled designs (*g* = 0.39). Jones, Woods & Guillaume (2016, *JOOP*, k = 17) found δ = 0.36 overall (skill-based δ = 0.28; affective δ = 0.51; results δ = 1.24), with *internal* coaches outperforming external ones and multisource feedback *reducing* effects. Sonesh et al. (2015) concurred coaching works but did not list included studies. A psychologically-informed meta-analysis (Wang et al., 2022, *JWAM*, k = 20) found goal attainment *g* = 1.29 and self-efficacy *g* = 0.59, with an *integrative* approach (*g* = 0.71) beating any single branded method — and, critically, *no* significant difference between popular coaching approaches. The most rigorous synthesis, de Haan & colleagues' RCT-only meta-analysis (2023, *Academy of Management Learning & Education*, 37 studies, 39 samples, n = 2,528), found *g* = 0.59, with notable publication bias. A key theme: number of sessions does not reliably moderate outcomes (Theeboom; Jones).

**Working alliance / relationship.** This is the most consistent process predictor. Graßmann, Schölmerich & Schermuly (2020, *Human Relations*) meta-analyzed the working-alliance–outcome relationship in coaching and found a significant positive association across outcome types, though heterogeneous. De Haan et al. (2013, *Consulting Psychology Journal*, 156 client–coach pairs) found client-rated working alliance, client self-efficacy, and perceived coach techniques all predicted outcome, with the alliance mediating the others. De Haan, Grant, Burger & Eriksson (2016, 1,895 client–coach pairs, 366 coaches, 34 countries) confirmed alliance and self-efficacy predict effectiveness while personality matching (MBTI) does not — and importantly, "the task and goal aspects of the working alliance were stronger predictors... than the bond aspects." Baron & Morin (2009) established the alliance as an outcome predictor in executive coaching. A wrinkle: de Haan, Molyn & Nilsson (2020) found alliance predicted success early in the contract but not strongly session-to-session, prompting the field to "think differently about active ingredients."

**Questioning technique.** The best experimental evidence concerns solution-focused vs. problem-focused questions. Grant & O'Connor (2010, pilot, N = 74) and Grant (2012, *Journal of Systemic Therapies*, randomized, N = 225) found both question types improved goal approach, but solution-focused questions produced significantly greater gains in positive affect, self-efficacy, and goal approach, and larger reductions in negative affect. This has replicated cross-culturally: Neipp et al. (2015/2016, Spanish, N = 204) found solution-focused questions produced significantly greater increases in self-efficacy, goal approach, and action steps; Theeboom et al. (2016, Dutch) found solution-focused questions raised positive affect and cognitive flexibility. Solms et al. (2022, *Frontiers in Psychology*, N = 183 medical residents) extended this to working professionals. The practical implication (Grant): real conversations mix both, but coaches should aim for a solution-focused *theme*. "Powerful/open questions" as such have less direct experimental testing; the robust finding is the solution-focus orientation.

**Goal-setting theory inside a live conversation.** Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory — specific, challenging goals, with commitment and feedback, drive performance — is one of the most replicated paradigms in I/O psychology, and goal-directed self-regulation is where coaching shows its largest effects (Theeboom *g* = 0.74; Wang goal attainment *g* = 1.29). But the theory has serious, well-documented limits. Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky & Bazerman (2009, "Goals Gone Wild," *Academy of Management Perspectives*) argue the benefits are overstated and catalog systematic side effects: narrowed focus that neglects non-goal areas, distorted risk preferences, increased unethical behavior, inhibited learning, corroded culture, and reduced intrinsic motivation — recommending goals be treated like "prescription-strength medication" requiring careful dosing, not an over-the-counter cure. A coaching-specific systematic review ("Of SMART, GROW and goals gone wild," Turner et al., 2020, *International Coaching Psychology Review*, 24 studies) found goal activities sometimes relate positively to outcomes and sometimes show no association, and noted "the scarcity of empirical research stands in contrast to the prominent role of goals in the coaching literature." Implication: goals help, but rigid/over-specified goal-setting in a coaching conversation can backfire; keep goals client-owned, comprehensive, and learning-oriented.

### 3. Evidence-Based Practice for the "Options" Stage

**The core risk: the coach anchors the client.** Advice-taking research (the Judge–Advisor System) consistently shows two things relevant to a coach generating options *for* a client. First, "egocentric discounting": people underweight others' advice (Yaniv & Kleinberger, 2000; a meta-analytic average weight-of-advice ≈ 0.39, Bailey et al., 2023). Second, and more dangerous for coaching, anchoring: Schultze, Mojzisch & Schulz-Hardt (2017, *Experimental Psychology*, four experiments) showed people cannot ignore even *explicitly useless* advice — it acts as an anchor that shifts their estimates regardless of quality. Translated to coaching: the moment a coach voices a suggestion in the Options stage, it anchors and contaminates the client's own solution set, narrows the range considered, and undermines the client ownership that predicts follow-through. This is the mechanism behind the "expert bias" problem.

**Idea-generation evidence.** The brainstorming literature strongly favors independent generation before interaction. Diehl & Stroebe (1987, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) identified "production blocking" — because only one person can speak at a time, individuals forget or suppress ideas — as the main reason interactive brainstorming groups produce fewer and lower-quality ideas than the same number of people working alone ("nominal groups"). Mullen, Johnson & Salas (1991) meta-analytically confirmed individuals outperform brainstorming groups. Nijstad, Stroebe & Lodewijkx (2003) traced blocking to disrupted cognitive organization of idea generation. Practical antidotes with evidence: nominal group technique and brainwriting (writing ideas independently), quantity-first instructions (quantity correlates highly with number of good ideas, r ≈ 0.82 in Diehl & Stroebe), and strict separation of idea generation from evaluation to avoid premature convergence/fixation.

**Directive vs. non-directive.** Coaching's own evidence (de Haan; Graßmann) shows specific techniques matter less than the alliance and the client's self-efficacy — and directive advice-giving risks both anchoring and reduced ownership. The synthesis for an AI Options stage: prompt the client to generate a *wide* set of self-authored options first (quantity target, no evaluation), use solution-focused prompts ("What else could you try?", "If you had no constraints...", "When has a version of this worked before?"), withhold the coach's own suggestions until the client's set is exhausted, and if information must be supplied, frame it as neutral world-of-work information rather than a recommended course of action.

### 4. Evidence-Based Practice for the "Will" Stage / Commitment

This is where psychology offers the coach the most robust, quantified tools.

**Implementation intentions (if-then plans).** Gollwitzer's construct — "If situation Y occurs, then I will do X" — bridges the gap between goal and action. Gollwitzer & Sheeran's (2006, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*) meta-analysis of 94 independent tests (N > 8,000) found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, *d* = 0.65, robust to publication-bias adjustment. The most recent and largest synthesis (Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer, 2024, *European Review of Social Psychology*, 642 tests) found effects across cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes (0.27 ≤ *d* ≤ 0.66), *larger* when plans used a genuine contingent if-then format, when motivation was high, and when the plan was rehearsed at least once.

**MCII / WOOP (mental contrasting with implementation intentions).** Oettingen's protocol pairs imagining the desired future, contrasting it with present obstacles, then forming if-then plans. Wang, Wang & Gai (2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*, 21 studies, 24 effect sizes, N = 15,907) found a small-to-medium effect (*g* = 0.336), stronger when delivered interactively (experimenter-guided *g* = 0.465) than via documents (*g* = 0.277) — directly relevant to an interactive AI coach. Grant has shown MCII and solution-focused questions produce comparable goal-directed gains.

**The intention–behavior gap.** Strong intentions are necessary but insufficient. Sheeran (2002) found intentions explain ~28% of variance in behavior, and experimental changes in intention produce only small-to-medium behavior change (Webb & Sheeran, 2006, R² ≈ .03) — people act on "good intentions" only about half the time. This is precisely the gap implementation intentions close.

**Progress monitoring / accountability.** Harkin et al. (2016, *Psychological Bulletin*, 138 studies, N = 19,951, RCTs only) found interventions prompting progress monitoring reliably increased monitoring frequency (*d* = 1.98) and promoted goal attainment (*d* = 0.40), with the effect on attainment mediated by monitoring frequency. Crucially, effects were *larger when progress was reported/made public and physically recorded* — direct support for accountability features and logging in an AI tool. Public and written commitment thus have specific empirical backing.

**Application in a coaching conversation:** end sessions by converting the chosen option into (1) a specific if-then plan naming the cue (time/place/event) and the response, (2) a brief mental-contrasting step surfacing the main obstacle and a coping if-then, and (3) a monitoring/accountability commitment that is recorded and, ideally, reported back.

### 5. Career-Coaching-Specific Research

**Job-search interventions (strongest career-specific evidence).** Liu, Huang & Wang (2014, *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(4), 1009–1041, 47 experimental/quasi-experimental studies) found that "the odds of obtaining employment were 2.67 times higher for job seekers participating in job search interventions compared to job seekers in the control group." The effect held only when interventions combined *both* skill development *and* motivation enhancement. The individual components carried strikingly large odds ratios: encouraging proactivity (OR = 5.88), promoting goal-setting (OR = 4.67), enlisting social support (OR = 4.26), improving self-presentation (OR = 3.40), teaching job-search skills (OR = 3.32), and boosting self-efficacy (OR = 3.25); effects were mediated by job-search skills, self-efficacy, and behaviors. Van Hooft and colleagues' job-search self-regulation work frames job search as a volitional, self-regulated, and stressful process, distinguishing job-search *behavior* self-efficacy (which sustains effort through setbacks) from job-search *outcome* self-efficacy (which can reduce effort). The classic JOBS program (Vinokur et al.) is the evidence-based template, replicated internationally (e.g., South Africa, Paver et al., 2020).

**Career counseling effectiveness generally.** Career interventions are moderately effective. Whiston, Li, Mitts & Wright (2017, *Journal of Vocational Behavior*, 57 studies) — a replication of Brown & Ryan Krane (2000) — found a weighted mean effect of *d* = 0.352, with career decision-making self-efficacy showing the largest effects (*d* ≈ 0.45); both number of sessions and hours mattered, and counselor support emerged as critical. Individual career counseling is the most effective modality per session; structured group interventions beat unstructured ones (Whiston et al., 2003); self-directed interventions are weakest.

**Critical ingredients (Brown & Ryan Krane).** The landmark finding is that five components drive career-intervention effectiveness, and effect sizes rise almost linearly with how many are combined: (1) written exercises, (2) individualized interpretation and feedback, (3) up-to-date world-of-work/occupational information, (4) modeling (exposure to models), and (5) attention to building support. Studies using none averaged *d* ≈ 0.22; one, two, or three components yielded roughly 0.45, 0.61, and 0.99 respectively. Whiston et al. (2017) partially replicated this — written exercises, individualized interpretation/feedback, and occupational information remained significant, but *modeling and building support did not* — and added three: counselor support, values clarification, and psychoeducation.

**Career construction / life design (Savickas) — honest appraisal.** The evidence here is asymmetric. The *measurement tool* is strongly validated: the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale (Savickas & Porfeli, 2012, *JVB*; 24 items across Concern, Control, Curiosity, Confidence) was validated across 13 countries, though it achieved metric but not scalar invariance (limiting cross-country mean comparisons); a reliability-generalization meta-analysis (Demirtaş-Zorbaz et al., 2026, 149 studies, N = 82,519) confirms generally high reliability. The *construct* of career adaptability is a strong predictor: Rudolph, Lavigne & Zacher (2017, *JVB*, 90 studies) found career adaptability associated with, and showing incremental predictive validity for, employability, promotability, income, satisfaction, engagement, lower turnover intentions, and performance. However — and this is the honest caveat — the evidence that Savickas's *interventions themselves* (Life Design counseling, the Career Construction Interview, "My Career Story") *cause* better outcomes is comparatively thin: dominated by case studies, qualitative process research, and small quasi-experimental pre-post designs with inconsistent results (several found no significant effect on career adaptability or maturity, e.g., Cardoso et al.), and few or no RCTs. The theory's empirical strength lies in construct validation and prediction, not demonstrated intervention efficacy. Broad career-intervention meta-analyses (Whiston; *d* ≈ 0.35) pool many orientations and are not specific to life design.

### 6. Networking Research — What Organizational-Behavior and Social-Capital Science Add

**Weak ties (Granovetter) — now causally tested.** Granovetter's (1973) "strength of weak ties" argued acquaintances, not close friends, deliver novel job information because they bridge otherwise disconnected social clusters. This was correlational for decades (and earlier large tests found a "paradox of weak ties" favoring strong ties). Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson & Aral (2022, *Science*, 377(6612), 1304–1310) provided the first large-scale causal test: multiple randomized experiments on LinkedIn's "People You May Know" algorithm across more than 20 million people over a five-year period, during which 2 billion new ties and 600,000 new jobs were created. Findings, with three refinements: (1) the relationship is *nonlinear* — "an inverted U-shaped relationship between tie strength and job transmission" such that the most job mobility came from *moderately weak* ties, described by co-author Sinan Aral as friends "with whom you share roughly 10 connections and seldom interact... still weak ties, but not the weakest ties" — not the very weakest ties; (2) results differed by tie-strength measure ("moderately weak ties [measured by mutual connections] and the weakest ties [measured by interaction intensity] created the most job mobility"); and (3) "whereas weak ties increased job mobility in more digital industries, strong ties increased job mobility in less digital industries." So the practitioner mantra "cultivate weak ties" is causally supported but should be tuned to *moderately* weak ties and industry.

**Structural holes / brokerage (Burt).** Burt's structural-holes theory holds that people who bridge disconnected groups gain information and control advantages; brokerage is associated with better ideas, promotions, and compensation. This underpins the practitioner advice to build a diverse, non-redundant network rather than a dense clique.

**Dormant ties (Levin, Walter & Murnighan).** Contrary to the assumption that unmaintained ties lose value, Levin, Walter & Murnighan (2011, *Organization Science*) had Executive MBA students reconnect with dormant contacts (out of touch 3+ years) for advice on a real project; outcomes compared favorably to current ties. Reconnected *strong* dormant ties uniquely combined the benefits of weak ties (novelty, efficiency) and strong ties (trust, shared perspective). Practical implication strongly supported: reactivating dormant ties is a high-yield, low-cost, under-used move — often better than either current contacts or cold new ones.

**Networking behavior and career outcomes (Wolff & Moser; Forret & Dougherty).** Wolff & Moser (2009, *Journal of Applied Psychology*), a three-year longitudinal study, found networking related to current salary and to the *growth rate* of salary over time, plus concurrent career satisfaction (though not satisfaction growth). Later work links internal vs. external networking to salary, promotions, and employer change, with gender differences (men's salary growth benefits more from internal networking, women's from external). This is among the better longitudinal support that networking *causes* career gains, not just correlates.

**Operational, personal, strategic networks (Ibarra & Hunter).** From a study of 30 emerging leaders, Ibarra & Hunter (2007, *Harvard Business Review*) distinguish operational (getting current work done), personal (external kindred spirits for development and referrals), and strategic (future-oriented, stakeholder, opportunity-spotting) networks, arguing leaders need all three and most neglect the strategic. This is a well-known practitioner-facing framework grounded in qualitative research, not experimental testing — useful for structuring a client's networking audit, but its causal claims are not experimentally established.

**Networking discomfort / "moral impurity" — contested.** Casciaro, Gino & Kouchaki (2014, *Administrative Science Quarterly*) reported that instrumental professional networking makes people feel morally "dirty" (invoking the "Macbeth effect"), reducing networking frequency and performance. This is widely cited but should be flagged: a well-powered preregistered replication (Ziani, 2022/2023) found a *null* result (d = .02) on both the main effect and the mechanism, and a related follow-up (Gino et al., 2020, *Journal of Applied Psychology*) was *retracted* amid data-integrity concerns. So the specific "networking feels dirty" claim is empirically shaky; the more durable, useful point is simply that many people find instrumental networking aversive and avoid it.

**What makes networking requests get answered (Flynn & Bohns).** Among the most useful and robust findings for a career coach: people dramatically underestimate others' willingness to help. Flynn & Lake [Bohns] (2008, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, six studies) found help-seekers underestimated compliance by as much as 50%; Bohns's (2016, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 25, 119–123) review reports that across 12 studies in which participants asked more than 14,000 strangers, "this error is robust... and substantial (on average, requesters underestimate compliance by 48%)," driven by "requesters' failure to appreciate the awkwardness of saying 'no' to a request." Zhao & Epley (2022, *Psychological Science*, N = 2,118) found people also underestimate how *positively* helpers feel when asked. Newark, Flynn & Bohns (2014) found people over-write off contacts after a single "no." Caveats: most studies used small favors among strangers on North American campuses, and they measure compliance, not whether helpers were glad afterward. Still, the direction is robust and directly coachable: encourage clients to ask more, ask more directly (and in person/synchronously), and not to disqualify contacts after a single refusal — including for dormant-tie reconnection and informational interviews.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build the container, but load it with evidence.** Use a GROW-style structure for familiarity and flow, but do not rely on GROW as the active ingredient. Prioritize the working alliance — specifically the *task and goal* agreement components (de Haan et al., 2016) — and reinforce client self-efficacy, which predicts outcomes. Benchmark to change: if user goal-attainment and self-efficacy measures are not moving, adjust technique before adding sessions (session number does not reliably drive outcomes).

**Stage 2 — Default to solution-focused questioning.** Bias the question library toward solution-focused prompts (exceptions, scaling, "miracle"/success questions, next-step questions), which have the clearest experimental support (Grant, 2012 and replications). Keep goals specific but client-owned, comprehensive, and learning-oriented to avoid "Goals Gone Wild" side effects; watch for tunnel vision, gaming, or ethical shortcuts as red flags that goals are over-specified.

**Stage 3 — In Options, make the client generate first and protect against coach-anchoring.** Prompt independent, quantity-first idea generation with no evaluation; only after the client's set is exhausted should the tool offer additional options, and then framed as neutral information, not a recommendation. This is the single most important design guardrail for an *AI* coach, because anchoring operates even with useless input (Schultze et al., 2017) and AI suggestions are especially likely to anchor. Threshold to change: if usage logs show clients predominantly selecting the tool's suggested options rather than self-generated ones, tighten the withholding rule.

**Stage 4 — In Will, always convert intent into an if-then plan plus monitoring.** Close every action step as an implementation intention (cue + response; *d* ≈ 0.65), add a mental-contrasting obstacle-coping if-then (WOOP), and set a recorded, reportable progress-monitoring commitment (Harkin et al., 2016 — public/recorded monitoring is measurably stronger). Rehearse the plan once in-session (rehearsal increases effect). Benchmark: track the intention–behavior gap directly (planned vs. completed actions); if follow-through lags, strengthen plan specificity and accountability features before adding content.

**Career-transition module.** For job seekers, combine skill-building AND motivation/self-efficacy work (interventions with only one underperform; both together ≈ 2.67× re-employment odds), and prioritize the highest-leverage components — proactivity, goal-setting, and enlisting social support (Liu et al., 2014). Embed the Brown & Ryan Krane critical ingredients that replicated: written exercises, individualized interpretation/feedback, and current world-of-work information, plus counselor-style support and values clarification. Use the CAAS construct (Concern, Control, Curiosity, Confidence) for assessment given its strong validation, but do not overclaim that life-design *interventions* have RCT-grade outcome evidence — they do not yet.

**Networking module.** Coach toward *moderately* weak ties and *dormant*-tie reactivation (both causally/experimentally supported) rather than only close contacts; tailor by industry (weak ties for digital fields, strong ties for less-digital ones). Have clients audit operational/personal/strategic networks (Ibarra & Hunter) as a structuring exercise. Most importantly, directly counteract the ~48% underestimation-of-help bias: prompt clients to make more, more-direct asks, prefer synchronous/in-person requests, and re-approach contacts after a "no." Do NOT lean on the "networking feels dirty" framing as established science (failed replication + retraction); instead normalize the aversion and lower the friction of asking.

## Caveats
- **Effect-size interpretation.** Coaching meta-analyses are inflated by within-subject designs and publication bias (explicitly flagged by de Haan et al., 2023, and Wang et al., 2022). Controlled-design estimates (*g* ≈ 0.36–0.59) are the honest baseline; headline figures like *g* = 0.66 or goal-attainment *g* = 1.29 come from weaker designs or specific outcomes.
- **Transportability.** Much questioning-technique evidence (Grant and replications) comes from single-session, self-coaching or undergraduate-writing-exercise paradigms, not full coach–client relationships; Solms et al. (2022) is a useful extension to professionals. Implementation-intention and progress-monitoring evidence is largely from health behaviors, though the mechanisms are domain-general.
- **GROW.** Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — GROW may work fine as a scaffold; there simply is almost no direct controlled test, and the one that exists (Scoular & Linley, 2006) found its goal-setting core made no significant difference.
- **Career-adaptability/life-design.** Strong construct and measurement evidence; weak and inconsistent *intervention* outcome evidence. Do not conflate the two.
- **Networking.** Ibarra & Hunter's typology is qualitative/theoretical, not experimentally tested; the Casciaro–Gino–Kouchaki "dirty networking" line has a failed preregistered replication and an adjacent retraction and should be cited only with that caveat; help-seeking studies are mostly small favors among strangers in North America and measure compliance, not downstream relationship effects.
- **Source hygiene.** This report prioritizes peer-reviewed journals (*JPSP*, *Psychological Bulletin*, *Journal of Vocational Behavior*, *Science*, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, *JOOP*, *Administrative Science Quarterly*, *AMLE*) and primary sources; coaching-vendor and blog material was used only for uncontested background (e.g., GROW's history) and flagged as such.
