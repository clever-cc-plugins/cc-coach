# Career Coaching Framework — Shared Reference

Shared reference for `career-coach`. Baseline framework used whether or not a
`career-plan`/`personal-branding-strategy` context file is loaded — when one is,
its content fills in what would otherwise be asked directly.

## GROW Model

**Structure a coaching conversation from goal to committed action**

What it is: A four-stage conversational structure — **Goal** (what does the person
want from this session, or from the situation they're raising), **Reality** (what's
actually true right now — skills, constraints, prior attempts), **Options** (what
could be done — generated by the person first, coach adds options only after),
**Will** (what will the person actually commit to, by when). The discipline: the
coach asks, the person answers — the coach does not hand the person their own plan.

Treat GROW as the container, not the cure. The four-stage shape is a useful,
memorable way to keep a conversation moving from vague to concrete, but the parts
of coaching with the strongest support are the ingredients loaded into each
stage — solution-focused questioning, protecting the person's own option
generation, and converting commitments into if-then plans with real follow-up.
Get those right and the exact stage order matters less; get them wrong and no
amount of GROW structure fixes it.

**Default to a solution-focused question style.** Across all four stages, prefer
questions oriented at what's wanted and what's already working over questions
oriented at causes and blame — "what would need to shift for you to prioritize
this?" lands better than "why haven't you done this yet?"; "what's worked before,
even partially?" surfaces more usable material than "what went wrong?". This
isn't about avoiding hard truths — Reality-stage questions can and should surface
real constraints — it's about the angle of approach. A problem-focused question
tends to pull the person into rumination and defensiveness; a solution-focused
one pulls them toward agency, even when the underlying fact is the same.

How to apply the four stages:

1. **Goal.** Ask what the person wants from this conversation — don't assume you
   already know from how the session opened. If a `career-plan` context file is
   loaded, you may ground this in one of its milestones ("Want to work through the
   milestone about X?") rather than asking cold — but still let the person confirm
   or redirect. Keep the goal specific enough to act on but owned by the person,
   not over-specified by you — a goal so narrow it becomes a box-ticking exercise
   tends to produce tunnel vision and gaming rather than real progress; if the
   conversation starts optimizing the letter of the goal over its point, loosen it.
   Distinguish early whether this is a **tactical** goal (find a specific job, get
   a specific promotion) or an **identity** goal (a transition into a different
   kind of work, a re-evaluation of what kind of professional they want to be) —
   a transition framed only as "find the next job" often needs the Goal and
   Reality stages to also make room for "what do I want to be true about who I am
   professionally," not just a faster action plan.
2. **Reality.** Ask what's true now relative to that goal — what's been tried,
   what's in the way, what resources exist. If `career-background` context is
   loaded, note it silently rather than re-asking; if not, ask directly here.
3. **Options.** Ask the person to generate options first, and keep asking for more
   before evaluating any of them — "what else could you try?", "if there were no
   constraints, what would you do?", "when has something like this worked before?"
   Resist the pull to jump in with a good idea the moment one occurs to you. This
   matters more for an AI coach than it might for a human one: a suggestion is
   cheap and fast for you to produce, which makes it especially tempting to offer
   early — but even an option the person immediately rejects still narrows what
   they generate next, because once a suggestion is on the table it's hard to
   un-hear. Only once the person's own list is genuinely exhausted (they've said
   something like "that's all I've got") should you add options they haven't
   named — and when you do, label them explicitly as your suggestion, not
   something they already thought of, so the ownership of the final choice stays
   theirs. The Networking domain below is a catalog for exactly this
   moment — a reserve of coach-suggested options, not a script to work through
   before the person has had their turn.
4. **Will.** Convert the chosen option into a concrete plan, not just a decision:
   - State it as an if-then plan naming the trigger and the response ("when I
     sit down Monday morning, I will send the first outreach message") rather
     than a bare intention ("I'll reach out more"). A vague "I'll think about it"
     is not a Will-stage answer — push once for the specific version before
     treating the stage as complete.
   - Ask what's most likely to get in the way, and get a coping response for it
     before the session ends ("if X happens, then I'll do Y instead") — don't
     assume the first plan survives contact with the first obstacle.
   - Ask for a confidence rating, 1-10, that they'll actually do it. A 7 or below
     is a signal, not a pass — it means something is still unresolved (a
     competing priority, an unclear next step, an unstated doubt), and it's
     cheaper to surface that now than to find out next session it didn't happen.
     Loop back into Options or Reality if the number is low, rather than closing
     the stage on a shaky commitment.
   - Land on how they'll track it, and whether they'll report back — a plan that
     gets written down and checked on holds up better than one that lives only
     in the conversation. This is what the contract's Step 5 save-prompt is for.
   - Rehearse it once before moving on — having the person say the plan back in
     their own words, out loud, makes it more likely to survive past the session.

When a `personal-branding-strategy` context file is loaded and the goal touches
visibility or positioning, reference its Why/How/What and key themes directly in
the Reality and Options stages rather than asking the person to restate their
brand.

## Adapting to What the Person Actually Needs

Rapport and a friendly tone are necessary but not sufficient — what predicts
whether a session actually moves someone forward is closer to _fit_ than to
warmth alone. Read, early, whether the person wants structure (a clear
next-step plan) or space to think out loud; whether they want to be challenged
on a plan or have it stress-tested gently; whether they respond better to
direct questions or to being given room to arrive at something themselves.
Adjust the pacing and directness of your questions to that read rather than
running one fixed coaching style regardless of who's in the conversation.

## Job Search as a GROW Options-Stage Domain

**What to offer when the Options stage touches an active job search**

When the goal is landing a specific job rather than a longer-term transition,
job-search-specific options work best combined, not chosen between. Search
tactics alone (polishing a resume, applying to more postings) tend to
underperform, as does confidence- or motivation-only coaching in isolation —
the two reinforce each other. If the person's Reality-stage account is heavy on
one and thin on the other (a great resume but low outreach volume, or a lot of
activity but low follow-through), name that imbalance before adding new Options
in whichever side is already strong.

Within that, a few components carry outsized weight and are worth prioritizing
in the Options stage over generic "apply more" suggestions: encouraging
proactive outreach rather than waiting on postings, turning the search into
specific goals with dates rather than an open-ended activity, and enlisting
people who can vouch for or introduce the person rather than searching alone.
The Networking domain below is the natural next stop once "enlist support"
becomes the chosen Option.

## Networking as a GROW Options-Stage Domain

**What to offer when the Options stage touches building relationships**

Networking comes up constantly in career GROW sessions — a job search, a
promotion push, finding a mentor, breaking into a new field. Offer these as
Options-stage suggestions, never as a fixed curriculum to march through — per
the Options-stage rule above, this is reserve material for once the person's
own list is exhausted, not an opening move.

**A tie only helps if it delivers something.** Before coaching someone toward a
specific contact, check what that contact would actually provide — new
information they don't already have, a resource, or a willingness to vouch for
them. A large network with none of these on offer isn't worth the energy of
expanding further; a smaller network that clearly provides one of the three is
worth deepening. Use this as the filter before adding "reach out to more
people" as an Option.

**Moderately-weak ties beat both extremes — tuned by field.** The old advice to
just "cultivate weak ties" is closer to "cultivate the _right kind_ of weak
tie": acquaintances with some mutual connections tend to open more doors than
either total strangers or very close contacts, because they sit at the seam
between the person's world and a different one. In more digital, fast-moving
fields, lean toward these bridge-type weaker ties; in more traditional or
relationship-driven fields, close ties carry more of the weight. When the
person is deciding who to prioritize reaching out to, this is a useful second
filter after the "what would they provide" one above.

**Internal vs. external networking serve different goals.** Building
relationships inside the person's current organization mainly pays off as
satisfaction and standing where they already are; building relationships
outside it mainly pays off as future mobility and optionality. If the
Goal-stage answer is "get ahead here," weight Options toward internal
relationship-building; if it's "keep my options open" or "find what's next,"
weight toward external. Don't let a generic "network more" Option go
unexamined — ask which of the two the person actually needs.

**Give first, don't keep score.** The single most load-bearing habit across this
domain: lead every relationship with something offered, not something asked for.
A person who reaches out only when they need something reads as transactional
even when they don't mean to. When the person's Option is "ask X for help," check
whether they have something to offer X first — even a small one (a relevant
article, an introduction, genuine interest in X's own work).

**Relationship Action Plan.** A concrete Will-stage tool: (1) name a goal 1-3
months out, (2) list specific people — not categories, actual names — who could
help reach it, (3) for each name, decide the outreach medium and, critically, what
to lead with generously rather than what to ask for. Offer this when the person's
Will-stage commitment is vague ("I'll network more") — it converts a vague
intention into named people and a specific next action per person.

**Anchor connectors.** In any group or event the person is already part of (a
team, an alumni group, a recurring meetup), there's usually one person who
connects to many _other_ circles the person doesn't have access to — identifiable
by noticing whose name keeps coming up in others' stories. Cultivating that one
relationship (not the whole room) often opens more doors than broad, shallow
outreach. Useful when the Option on the table is "attend more networking events"
— ask who at events they've already attended stood out this way, before adding a
new event to the plan.

**Dormant ties are the highest-yield move on this list — treat reconnection as
a skill, not a formality.** A contact who's gone quiet for years is not a dead
end; reconnected long-lapsed contacts often outperform both a person's current
network (fresher perspective, less redundant information) and a cold
introduction (existing trust, shared history) at once. But reactivation fails
in a predictable way when three things aren't lined up first, so coach through
them before the person reaches out: do they think the other person actually
remembers them with any warmth (not just recognizes the name); do they have an
honest, comfortable answer for why they lost touch, so the catch-up doesn't
open with an apology neither party needs; and do both sides seem likely to see
the relationship the same way now (both open to it mattering again, not one
side treating it as an obligation). When these line up, reconnecting is usually
a stronger first move than adding a new contact — offer it before "attend more
events" or "meet more new people," not after.

**Light, regular touchpoints ("pinging").** Sustaining a network doesn't require
big asks — a short, genuine check-in (sharing something relevant to that person,
congratulating a visible win) keeps a relationship warm without demanding their
time.

**Contacts are not relationships.** A name in an address book or a LinkedIn
connection is a contact; a relationship exists only where real time has been
invested. If the person's Reality-stage account of their network is a long
contact list but few people they could actually call for help, name that gap
directly — the Options stage should prioritize deepening a few relationships over
adding more contacts.

**Correct the "they probably won't want to help" assumption directly.** People
routinely and substantially underestimate how willing others are to help with a
direct, specific ask, and underestimate how good it feels to the other person
to be asked. This isn't an Option to offer — it's a belief to correct when it
surfaces. If the person's Reality-stage account includes "I don't want to
bother them" or a plan to reach out indirectly because a direct ask feels
presumptuous, say so plainly: the discomfort is real but the prediction behind
it is usually wrong, and a single "no" from one contact doesn't mean the next
person will say no too — don't let one lapsed reply write off further asks.

**For an introvert-identified person — offer the one-on-one mindset explicitly,
don't default to group-networking advice:**

1. Name specific people 1-2 levels above or adjacent to the person's role they'd
   like to know better — not a room full of strangers.
2. Ask each one separately for a short one-on-one (a "quick coffee" or its remote
   equivalent) rather than a group setting.
3. Prepare one genuine question or a real reason for the meeting before it happens
   ("I'm intrigued by X, would love your take").
4. Ask directly: "How can I be of help to you?" — and have a ready answer for when
   they ask it back.
5. Follow up with a specific, personal thank-you, not a generic one.
6. Budget explicit recovery time after any high-exposure networking event or
   interview day — treat it as a real cost, not a discipline failure, and plan
   around it rather than pushing through it.

This isn't a lesser version of group networking — for a person who finds group
settings draining, it's the higher-yield version, because deep one-on-one
relationships are where introverts are most effective. Never frame it as a
consolation path to the "real" (extroverted) way of networking.

## Live Rapport-Building for the Options Stage

**How the person actually behaves in the room, once a networking Option becomes
a real conversation**

The networking section above covers _what_ to do — who to reach out to and how
to open the relationship. This section is about _how the person shows up_ once
they're actually talking to someone, whether that's the coffee meeting from the
one-on-one mindset above or any other live conversation the Options stage
produced.

- **Genuine interest before being interesting.** Asking real questions about
  the other person and listening to the answers builds more goodwill than any
  amount of being impressive. Coach toward this directly when the person's plan
  for a meeting is mostly about what they'll say about themselves.
- **Use the other person's name.** Small, concrete, and reliably underused —
  a person's name, used naturally in conversation, registers as attention paid.
- **Talk in terms of the other person's interests.** Before a meeting, the
  person should be able to answer: what does the other person care about, and
  where does that overlap with what I bring? A meeting planned only around the
  asker's own agenda undersells the connection.
- **Let the other person feel genuinely important — and mean it.** This fails
  immediately if it reads as technique; the underlying move is finding what's
  actually admirable or interesting about the other person and saying so
  specifically, not generic flattery.
- **Avoid direct criticism or argument in a relationship-building conversation.**
  Even when the person disagrees with something the other party says, a
  networking conversation is rarely the place to win the point — coach toward
  finding the part they can genuinely agree with first.

How to apply: when the person's Reality-stage account of a past networking
attempt describes it going flat or feeling one-sided, check it against this
list — usually the gap is one of "talked mostly about myself" or "didn't ask a
real question and listen to the answer," not a lack of opportunities.

## When the Goal Is a Long Haul, Not a Single Action

If the person's Goal is an extended challenge (a career pivot, a promotion push
spanning many months, rebuilding after a setback) rather than a discrete next
action, use `life-coaching-framework.md`'s **Building Endurance for a Sustained
Challenge** section (the Focus/Agility/Coordination/Endurance framework) instead
of trying to force it into a single Will-stage commitment.
