# Presentation Coaching Framework — Shared Reference

Shared reference for `presentation-coach`. Three parts: a rubric for structuring
what to look at, a feedback model for how to say it, and evidence-based modules for
anxiety, delivery mechanics, and rehearsal — the parts of coaching a talk that a
rubric alone doesn't cover.

## Presentation Quality Rubric

**What to assess, in order**

1. **Message clarity** — can the core point be stated in one sentence? If the
   person can't state it in one breath, the talk doesn't have one yet.
2. **Structure and cognitive load** — does it open with something that earns
   attention (a claim, question, or story — not an agenda slide), build in an
   order the audience can follow, and close on the message rather than trailing
   into Q&A? A listening audience can't re-read a confusing sentence the way a
   reader can — it consumes the talk at the speaker's pace, not its own. That
   makes signposting and previews load-bearing, not decorative: name where the
   talk is going before it gets there, mark transitions explicitly ("that's the
   problem — here's what we did about it"), and cap the number of major points
   a listener has to hold at once at three or so. A structurally sound talk with
   too many simultaneous ideas still overloads the audience; treat "how much is
   the listener holding in their head right now" as its own check, separate from
   whether the ordering is logical. If slides are part of the talk, this is
   where to check them: dense, text-heavy slides read aloud verbatim add load
   rather than removing it — one idea per slide, minimal on-screen text, and no
   decorative detail that doesn't serve the point being made in that moment.
3. **Evidence** — are claims backed by something concrete an audience member
   could later repeat to someone else? Reward a concrete story or example
   **and** supporting data over either alone — the two persuade through
   different, complementary routes, and a talk that leans on only one is
   leaving persuasive weight on the table. Watch for data dumping: a statistic
   without an explanation of why it matters to this audience doesn't land as
   evidence, it lands as noise.
4. **Delivery — vocal** — pacing, filler words, pauses, vocal variety. Only
   assess this from what the person actually reports or reads aloud — never
   invent delivery notes for a talk you haven't heard. See "Filler Words,
   Pacing, and Vocal Delivery" below for what's actually worth coaching here
   versus what's folklore.
5. **Delivery — visual** — gestures, eye contact/camera presence, and above all
   **congruence**: whether the vocal and visual signals match what the words
   are claiming. A confident claim delivered with a hesitant voice or defensive
   posture reads as more hesitant than the words alone — audiences weight the
   nonverbal channel most heavily exactly when it conflicts with the verbal
   one. Coach toward alignment between what's said and how it's said, not
   toward "more gestures" or "more eye contact" as raw quantity — a gesture
   that visually depicts the point being made (hands shaping a "growing" curve
   while saying "this kept growing") does real work; a gesture that's just
   motion doesn't.
6. **Audience fit** — is the vocabulary, depth, and framing right for who's
   actually in the room? If a `personal-branding-strategy` context file is
   loaded, check the talk's message against its Why/How/What — a talk that
   contradicts the person's stated brand themes is a fit problem worth naming.

Work through the rubric in this order — message clarity first, since a
structural or delivery fix on an unclear message just polishes the wrong thing.

**A caveat worth naming for webinars specifically:** delivery techniques that
lean on presence and charisma (illustrative gestures, vocal variety, moral
conviction) show a large effect on how a speaker is rated in person but a
near-zero effect in virtual or camera-mediated settings. Don't coach a webinar
speaker as if the room reads their gestures the way an in-person audience
would — for that context, weight structure, clarity, and vocal delivery over
visual presence, since the camera flattens most of what visual presence does.

## SBI Feedback Model (Situation-Behavior-Impact) — a Structure, Not a Guarantee

**How to phrase feedback so it's usable, not just true — and what SBI can and
can't be trusted to do on its own**

What it is: Feedback in three parts — **Situation** (when/where, specific, not
"often"), **Behavior** (what was observed or reported, factual, not a character
judgment), **Impact** (what effect it had or would have on the audience). SBI is
a practitioner framework from the Center for Creative Leadership, not a
peer-reviewed, experimentally validated method — no controlled study has
compared it against alternatives specifically for speaking feedback. Use its
_structure_ because it reliably does one thing well: it forces feedback to
name an observable behavior instead of a trait judgment ("you read the agenda
slide word for word" instead of "you seemed unprepared"). That's worth keeping
regardless of SBI's own evidence status, because it's also what the actual
governing research on feedback requires.

**The rule that governs what SBI's three slots actually get filled with:**
feedback that stays on the task and the specific behavior improves
performance; feedback that drifts toward the person's traits or self-image
tends not to, and drops performance outright in a large minority of cases. So
every Situation-Behavior-Impact note should describe a specific moment and a
specific action — never a pattern framed as "you always" or a trait framed as
"you're not." If a piece of feedback can't be anchored to a specific situation
and observable behavior, it isn't ready to give yet — go back and find the
moment it's actually about.

**Pair SBI with a forward-looking prompt, especially during rehearsal.** SBI
describes what happened; it doesn't, by itself, tell the person what to do
differently. After naming the Situation-Behavior-Impact, add one concrete,
next-attempt suggestion — "try scripting only your first and last lines so you
can hold eye contact through the opening" — rather than leaving the person to
infer the fix from the impact alone. This matters more during active rehearsal
than in a one-time post-mortem: a rehearsal loop that only diagnoses the last
attempt without pointing at the next one wastes the repetition.

**Source attribution:** the Situation-Behavior-Impact structure originates with
the Center for Creative Leadership's _Feedback That Works_ (Weitzel, 2000; SBI
is a CCL trademark). Its governing evidence — that feedback works best when
kept task- and behavior-focused rather than directed at the self, and that
feedback interventions decrease performance in a substantial share of cases
when they don't — is Kluger & DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis in _Psychological
Bulletin_ and Hattie & Timperley's 2007 review in _Review of Educational
Research_. The forward-looking pairing draws on the same task-vs-self logic
that motivates Marshall Goldsmith's "feedforward" practice. These are stated
directly here as coaching guidance, not paraphrased from any single paper's
narrative.

## Filler Words, Pacing, and Vocal Delivery

**What's actually worth coaching here, and what's overstated folklore**

**Filler words aren't a defect to zero out.** "Um" and "uh" are delay signals
speakers produce while planning what comes next, and they sometimes measurably
help listeners — a preceding "uh" can speed up how fast a listener recognizes
the word that follows. A low, steady rate of fillers doesn't damage perceived
effectiveness; a genuinely high rate does. Coach toward reducing _excessive_
filler density, not toward eliminating every instance — "zero fillers" is not
the target and chasing it usually just adds a new source of anxiety during
delivery.

**The trainable substitute is the silent pause, not suppression.** When a
person wants to cut filler frequency, the working technique is replacing the
impulse to fill dead air with a deliberate silent pause instead — it serves
the same cognitive-planning function for the speaker without the perceived
cost, and reads to an audience as intentional emphasis rather than hesitation.
Telling someone to "just stop saying um" doesn't address why the filler
happened in the first place (usually planning load or insufficient
preparation on that specific passage) and rarely works on its own.

**There's no single correct pace — variation and audience stance matter more
than a target words-per-minute number.** A faster, more fluent baseline
generally reads as more confident and credible, but the effect flips with the
audience's stance: a skeptical or hostile audience benefits from a slower pace
that gives them room to counterargue less; an already-agreeing audience
benefits from more time to elaborate on why they agree. Coach a moderate,
fluent baseline with real variation — genuinely slowing down at a key point,
pausing after it — over a fixed rate.

**Don't teach the 7-38-55 rule.** The claim that words are "only 7% of
communication" while tone and body language carry the rest is a documented
misreading of a narrow 1967 study that measured only how listeners judge
_liking_ from single spoken words when the verbal and nonverbal channels
actively _conflict_ — it was never a general finding about communication, and
its own author has said so. The real, narrower finding worth keeping is about
congruence: when what's said and how it's said disagree, audiences weight the
nonverbal signal more heavily when reading the speaker's real attitude. That's
a reason to coach alignment between message and delivery (see rubric item 5),
not a reason to treat body language as dominating content in general.

**Source attribution:** the filler-word findings (delay-signal function,
listener benefit, and the reduction-not-elimination threshold) draw on Clark &
Fox Tree's 2002 psycholinguistic work in _Cognition_ and Fox Tree's 2001 study
in _Memory & Cognition_; the pacing-and-audience-stance finding draws on
Miller, Maruyama, Beaber & Valone (1976) and its elaboration-likelihood
qualification by Smith & Shaffer (1991); the 7-38-55 correction draws on
Mehrabian's own stated scope for his 1967 studies with Wiener and Ferris. These
are stated directly here as coaching guidance rather than paraphrased from any
single paper's narrative.

## Public Speaking Anxiety

**What measurably helps — and the two pieces of common advice that don't**

**Reappraisal beats "calm down."** Telling an anxious speaker to relax asks
them to suppress a high-arousal state, which is hard; relabeling that same
arousal as excitement instead is easier, because anxiety and excitement are
both high-arousal states and only differ in whether the person reads them as
threat or opportunity. Saying "I'm excited" (even just internally, right
before starting) measurably outperforms trying to calm down — speakers who
reframe this way are rated as more persuasive, competent, and confident.
Coach the reframe, not the suppression, when a person names pre-talk nerves.

**Practice alone doesn't reliably help someone who's genuinely high-anxiety.**
This is a real exception to "just rehearse more": speakers with high trait
anxiety about public speaking don't consistently use rehearsal time well, and
simply telling them to practice more can miss the actual mechanism that helps,
which is repeated _exposure_ — real or simulated — that lets the feared
outcome fail to materialize enough times to update the underlying threat
belief. If a person's anxiety sounds more like dread than ordinary nerves,
graded, repeated exposure (increasingly realistic run-throughs, a practice
audience, a recorded rehearsal watched back) is the better lever than another
solo read-through.

**Boundary.** This is coaching on ordinary performance anxiety, not treatment
for a clinical anxiety condition. If what surfaces sounds like it goes beyond
situational nerves before a talk — panic that doesn't track with the
speaking context specifically, or avoidance that's shutting down the person's
work more broadly — say so plainly and point toward a qualified professional
rather than continuing to coach through it.

**Source attribution:** the reappraisal finding is Alison Wood Brooks's 2014
study in the _Journal of Experimental Psychology: General_; the
practice-doesn't-reliably-help-high-anxiety-speakers finding and the exposure
mechanism are from Graham Bodie's 2010 review in _Communication Education_.
These are stated directly here as coaching guidance rather than paraphrased
from either paper's narrative.

## Rehearsal That Actually Improves a Talk

**What separates useful rehearsal from wasted repetition**

**Coached review is the active ingredient — not the recording itself.**
Watching a video of yourself, unguided, doesn't reliably improve a talk and
can make pre-talk anxiety worse rather than better; speakers tend to fixate on
things that don't matter and miss the things that do. What actually works is
a video review paired with structured, task-focused observations — someone
(or something) pointing at specific moments and naming what happened there.
This is the concrete reason a coaching conversation earns its place over
just re-watching a recording alone: the coaching layer is the part the
evidence says matters, not the raw footage.

**Rehearse a sub-skill, not the whole talk, on most passes.** Running the
entire talk start-to-finish every time plateaus quickly. A rehearsal pass
that targets one specific thing — just the opening 60 seconds, just the
transition into the data section, just the close — with feedback after that
one thing, produces more improvement per rehearsal than another full
read-through. Suggest a specific sub-skill for the next rehearsal pass rather
than "run it again."

**Extemporaneous beats memorized.** A fully memorized script tends to read as
memorized even to viewers who can't hear the words — it flattens vocal
variety and reads as less charismatic and less credible than a talk delivered
from a strong outline the speaker knows well enough to say differently each
time. Coach toward knowing the structure and the key lines (the opening, the
close, the one line that has to land) cold, and leaving the connective tissue
extemporaneous, rather than toward word-for-word memorization of the whole
thing.

**Source attribution:** the coached-vs-unguided video review finding draws on
Bodie's 2010 review and Liu & Aryadoust's 2022 comparative study in _Studies
in Language Assessment_; the sub-skill rehearsal principle draws on the
deliberate-practice research tradition (Ericsson) as it applies to bounded
domains rather than whole-performance repetition; the
extemporaneous-over-memorized observation is drawn from TED-talk delivery
analyses. These are stated directly here as coaching guidance rather than
paraphrased from any single source's narrative.

## Persuasion & Pre-Suasion for Live Talks

**How to make the message land, not just be correct**

The Message Clarity and Evidence steps of the rubric establish _what_ the talk
says. This section is about how audiences actually come to believe and act on
it — a live, spoken adaptation of the same research-based persuasion
mechanisms `cc-content`'s `persuasion-principles.md` applies to written
content. Use it during the Evidence and Structure passes of the rubric, and
always name the choice rather than applying it silently — the person coaching
a talk needs to understand _why_ a suggested change works, not just that it
does.

**Confident delivery can't rescue a weak argument — and can backfire trying.**
Vocal and visual confidence genuinely increase persuasion, but mainly by
making a strong argument land harder; when the underlying reasoning is weak,
confident delivery can trigger more scrutiny rather than less, because it
raises the bar the argument then fails to clear. If the Evidence pass surfaces
a genuinely thin argument, coach the argument first — no amount of delivery
polish substitutes for it, and coaching delivery alone on a weak point risks
making the weakness more visible, not less.

**Applying persuasion principles to a talk (not a page):**

- **Reciprocity** — open with something genuinely useful to the audience (a
  reframe, a surprising number, a checklist) before asking for their attention
  or buy-in on the harder point.
- **Commitment & Consistency** — get a small, visible agreement early (a show
  of hands, a rhetorical question the audience silently answers yes to) before
  the talk's larger ask; people who've already nodded once resist the
  follow-on ask less.
- **Social Proof** — named, specific examples ("three teams I've worked with
  this quarter...") land harder live than a claimed statistic the audience
  can't verify in the moment.
- **Authority** — establish it early and specifically (a concrete credential
  or result, not a title recited from a slide), then let it recede — a talk
  that keeps re-asserting authority reads as insecure.
- **Scarcity** — only use this for a talk with a genuine constraint (a real
  deadline, a real capacity limit); audiences read manufactured urgency in a
  live room faster than in text, because they can watch the speaker's
  certainty.
- **Unity** — strongest live persuasion lever available to a speaker in the
  room: naming the shared context ("everyone in this room has felt...") does
  more work live than it can on a page, because the audience can see each
  other's reactions confirm it.

**Pre-suasive opening (the first 30-60 seconds):** What the audience is
attending to right before the core message lands changes how they interpret
it. Before drafting the opening line, decide what you want the audience primed
toward — a problem (makes the solution feel relevant), a shared identity
(makes a Unity appeal land), a surprising fact (keeps attention open longer)
— and open there rather than with logistics or an agenda slide. Coach the
person to state this choice out loud during rehearsal: "I'm opening with the
problem because the audience needs to feel the stakes before they'll care
about the fix."

**Ethical use, live version:** the same two tests from
`persuasion-principles.md` apply, sharpened by the fact that a live audience
can see the speaker: would the audience, fully understanding the technique,
still want the outcome? And does the technique survive the speaker being
watched closely while using it? A pre-suasive opener or an authority claim
that only works because the audience can't see the speaker's face is a
text-only tactic — flag it as a bad fit for a live talk rather than porting it
over.

**Source attribution:** the principles and mechanisms in this section
(reciprocity, commitment & consistency, social proof, authority, scarcity,
unity, pre-suasion, attention priming) are drawn from the same research-based
work as `cc-content`'s `persuasion-principles.md` — most prominently Robert
Cialdini's _Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion_ and _Pre-Suasion: A
Revolutionary Way to Influence and Persuade_. The confidence-can't-rescue-a-
weak-argument caveat is drawn separately from vocal-confidence persuasion
research (Guyer et al.'s work on paralinguistic confidence cues and their
interaction with argument strength). The underlying mechanisms and named
effects are not copyrightable; the specific prose and case stories in the
Cialdini books are. This section reformulates the operational essence for
live, spoken delivery — it does not reproduce or paraphrase passages from
either book, and states the confidence/argument-strength finding directly
rather than paraphrasing any single paper's narrative.
