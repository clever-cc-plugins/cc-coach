# Life Coaching Framework — Shared Reference

Shared reference for `life-coach`. Two parts: a diagnostic for finding where to
focus, and a conversational structure for turning that focus into action.

## Wheel of Life

**Find where attention is actually needed before setting a goal**

What it is: A satisfaction check across life domains — typically Career, Finances,
Health, Relationships, Personal Growth, Fun & Recreation, Physical Environment, and
Contribution/Community (adjust the list slightly if the person names a domain that
doesn't fit these, e.g. Spirituality or Parenting). For each domain, the person
rates current satisfaction 1-10. Low-scoring domains the person also cares about are
the ones worth a coaching conversation; a low score in a domain the person genuinely
doesn't value is not automatically a problem to fix.

How to apply: (1) Ask the person to rate each of the 8 domains 1-10 — quick, gut
reaction, not overthought. (2) Ask which 1-2 domains, if raised by two points, would
most improve how their life feels overall — this is often not the lowest score. (3)
Use their answer, not the raw lowest number, to pick the session's focus. (4) Only
run the full wheel on a first session or when the person is unsure what to work on;
if they arrive with a specific domain already named, skip straight to GROW below.

## GROW Model

**Turn the chosen focus into a committed action**

Same four-stage structure as `career-coaching-framework.md`'s GROW section — Goal,
Reality, Options, Will — applied here to whichever life domain the Wheel of Life (or
the person directly) identified as the focus, without assuming a career or
professional frame. Ask what the person wants (Goal), what's true now (Reality),
have them generate options before you add any (Options), and press for a specific
committed action and timeframe (Will).

## Turning Commitments into Follow-Through

**Strengthen the Will stage when the person's history is "I commit, then it slips"**

A GROW session's Will stage produces a commitment; it doesn't guarantee the person
executes it once the conversation ends. When the person's Reality-stage account
suggests this is a recurring pattern (good intentions, poor follow-through), offer
these — as Options, not as a lecture on productivity systems.

**Make the next action physically concrete, not a project.** A vague commitment
("work on my finances") is not something a person can actually start doing — only
a specific, visible next physical action is ("call the bank Tuesday at 10am"). If
a Will-stage commitment still names an outcome rather than an action, push once
more: "What's the very next physical thing you'd do to move that forward?" A
commitment that's actually a whole project in disguise will quietly fail the same
way it always has.

**The two-minute rule.** If the committed next action would take less than two
minutes, the person should do it during the session or immediately after, not add
it to a list. Reserve deferred commitments for genuinely larger actions.

**Capture it outside their head.** A commitment that lives only in memory competes
with everything else demanding attention and tends to lose. Ask where the person
will record it — a note, a calendar entry, wherever they already trust — before
ending the session. This isn't about prescribing a specific productivity system;
it's about confirming the commitment has a home outside the conversation.

**80/20: which 20% of effort is actually driving the result they want.** When a
person's Reality stage describes broad, scattered effort with little visible
progress in the chosen domain, ask what small subset of what they're already doing
produces most of the value they care about — and what could be dropped or reduced
without much loss. Useful for domains like Fun & Recreation or Personal Growth
where the person has taken on more commitments than they can sustain.

**Use a short deadline to force focus, not to punish.** A commitment given a tight,
specific timeframe ("by Friday") gets treated with more focus than the same
commitment given a loose one ("sometime this month") — open-ended time tends to
get filled with lower-value activity regardless of how much is actually available.
When the person's Will-stage timeframe is vague or distant, offer a tighter one and
ask if it's realistic, rather than accepting "eventually."

**Source attribution:** the concepts in this section (capture/clarify/next-action,
the two-minute rule, and the effort-vs-result and deadline-focus effects) are
drawn from David Allen's _Getting Things Done_ and Timothy Ferriss's _The 4-Hour
Work Week_ (drawing on Vilfredo Pareto's 80/20 distribution and C. Northcote
Parkinson's observation on task expansion). The underlying techniques and named
effects are not copyrightable; the specific prose and case examples in those books
are. This section reformulates the operational essence as coaching guidance for
